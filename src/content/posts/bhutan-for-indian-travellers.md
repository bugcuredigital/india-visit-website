Bhutan is the easiest foreign country for an Indian traveller to enter and one
of the most rewarding, and the two facts are connected: the country controls
tourism deliberately, and the arrangements it makes for its neighbours are
different from those it makes for everyone else. Here is what actually applies
to you, and what to decide before you book.

*Entry rules and fees change. Everything below reflects the position at the
time of writing; we confirm the current requirements with you before you
travel.*

## The permit, not a visa

Indian nationals do not need a visa for Bhutan. What you need is an **entry
permit**, issued against a valid passport or a voter ID card, and arranged
either online in advance or on arrival at the border or at Paro airport.
Children travel on a birth certificate or passport. The permit covers Paro and
Thimphu; travel beyond them — to Punakha, for instance — needs a route permit,
which is a formality your guide handles.

We arrange the permit as part of the booking, so from your side it is a matter
of sending us a scan of the document you will travel on.

## The daily fee

Every visitor to Bhutan pays a **Sustainable Development Fee** for each night
in the country. It is charged per person per night, at a substantially lower
rate for Indian nationals than for other nationalities, and it is the mechanism
behind the "high value, low volume" tourism the country is known for — the
reason the dzongs are not crowded and the valleys still look like the
photographs.

The rate is set by the Bhutanese government and has changed more than once in
recent years, so we do not print a figure here. We confirm the current rate
before you book and build it into the quotation, so there is nothing to pay at
the border.

## Air or road

**By air**, you fly directly into Paro from Kolkata or Guwahati on Druk Air or
Bhutan Airlines. The flight is short — around an hour to ninety minutes — and
the approach, weaving between Himalayan peaks into a narrow valley, is one of
the most dramatic in the world. It is the way to arrive if the budget and the
dates allow.

**By road**, you enter at Phuntsholing, the Bhutanese town opposite Jaigaon in
West Bengal. Jaigaon is five to six hours by car from Bagdogra or Siliguri, or
about five from Guwahati, and from Phuntsholing it is a scenic but winding
five to six hours up to Thimphu or Paro. The road route is a full day each way,
but it costs less, it suits travellers starting in the North East or the
Darjeeling hills, and it shows you the country changing from the plains up
through the foothills in a way the flight cannot.

Many of our Bhutan journeys are built to run one way in and the other out.

## Money

The Ngultrum is pegged one-to-one with the Indian rupee, and rupees are
accepted almost everywhere. ATMs exist in the towns but are not something to
rely on; carry cash in mixed denominations, as some shops decline larger notes.

## When to go

March to May and September to November, for clear views of the mountains.
The autumn months are also when the tsechu festivals fall in several of the
dzongs, which — if the dates line up — are worth planning a trip around.
