Bali is the one Hindu island in the largest Muslim country on earth, and for
an Indian traveller it is also one of the easiest foreign trips to arrange:
the visa is issued on arrival, the season is straightforward, and the island is
small enough that the decisions are about where to stay rather than how to
get around. Here is what to know before booking.

*Visa rules change, sometimes at short notice. Everything below reflects the
position at the time of writing; we confirm the current requirements with you
before you travel.*

## The visa on arrival

Indian passport holders are eligible for Indonesia's **visa on arrival**,
issued at Denpasar's Ngurah Rai airport and valid for **thirty days**. It is
paid for at the counter, and an electronic version can be applied for online
before you fly, which shortens the queue at the other end.

What you need to carry:

- a passport with **at least six months' validity** from the date of arrival,
  with a blank page;
- a **confirmed onward or return ticket** — immigration does ask;
- the fee, in a form the counter accepts, or the e-visa confirmation if you
  applied ahead.

The thirty days can be extended once from inside Indonesia if your plans
change, but that is an errand rather than a formality. If you might stay
longer, tell us before you go.

## Getting there

There are no reliable direct flights from most Indian cities. The typical
routing runs through **Singapore, Kuala Lumpur or Jakarta** into Denpasar, and
the whole journey — with the connection — is a long day. It is worth choosing
the connection for the layover as much as the price: Singapore's airport is
the easiest place in the region to spend three hours.

## When to go

Bali's year divides simply into a dry season and a wet one.

**April to October** is the dry season and the reliable window — warm, clear
and comfortable, with the coolest and driest months in the middle. It is also
the busiest, particularly July and August, when Australian and European
holidays coincide.

**November to March** is the monsoon. Rain tends to come in heavy afternoon
bursts rather than all day, and the island is green and quiet, but **December
to February** is the wettest stretch and the one to avoid if your dates are
flexible.

For an Indian traveller working around the school calendar, **May and June**
are the sweet spot: dry, not yet at peak, and the rice terraces at their
greenest after the rains.

## Where to base yourself

The island rewards restraint. The traffic is worse than the map suggests, and
the travellers who enjoy Bali most are the ones who chose two bases and stayed
put rather than trying to circle it. Our itineraries generally split between
**Ubud** — the rice terraces at Tegallalang and Jatiluwih, the water temples,
the craft villages — and **one coast** chosen for what you want from it:
Seminyak for restaurants and beach clubs, Uluwatu for the cliffs and the surf,
or somewhere quieter.

A private car with a driver is by far the easiest way to move between the two,
and it costs less than you would expect.

## Money

The currency is the Indonesian Rupiah. Cards are widely accepted in Ubud and
Seminyak; carry cash for smaller towns, warungs and temple donations, and
change it at the airport or a bank rather than a street counter.
